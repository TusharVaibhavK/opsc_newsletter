Most GSoC advice online is written to make it sound easy. It isn't, and knowing that early is an advantage: it tells you where to spend your time.

## The numbers

In 2026, Google Summer of Code accepted **1,141 contributors from 15,245 applicants** across 184 organizations ([Google Open Source Blog](https://opensource.googleblog.com/2026/04/the-journey-begins-meet-the-2026-gsoc-contributors.html)). That's about **7.5%** overall, and roughly **6 slots per org**.

The average hides a huge spread. Famous orgs with beginner-friendly stacks get hundreds of proposals for a handful of slots. Niche orgs (databases, compilers, scientific computing, Java and Go infrastructure) sometimes get a few dozen.

## What mentors actually select for

Mentors are choosing someone they'll work with for three months. They're asking:

1. **Will this person finish?** Steady activity over months is the strongest evidence. Four months of regular contributions beats four frantic weeks in March.
2. **Can they work in our codebase?** Merged pull requests that touch real logic, not just docs, show you can.
3. **Do they communicate well?** Clear questions in public channels, prompt replies to review, and summaries of decisions.
4. **Is the proposal grounded?** A plan that names real files, real functions and a believable timeline, ideally with a small proof of concept.

Notice what's not on the list: the total number of pull requests.

## What quietly disqualifies people

- **Typo and whitespace PRs in bulk.** Maintainers see them as farming. One documentation fix is fine; ten are a signal you're optimising the wrong thing.
- **“Please assign me” on many issues at once**, then disappearing.
- **Private messages to mentors** asking for help or for your proposal to be reviewed. Ask in public channels; it's faster and it's visible.
- **AI-generated work you can't explain.** Many orgs now publish AI-use policies. Follow them, and make sure you can defend every line you submit. Mentors can tell.
- **Starting in March.** By then, the people who started in October have months of history with the maintainers.

## What this means for your plan

- Start now, and pick an org where your hours count: one with a technical barrier you can climb, not the most famous name.
- Aim for **6–10 merged PRs in one or two orgs by mid-March**, with at least two that touch real backend or ML logic.
- Go deep in one community instead of shallow across five.

## If you don't get in

Most applicants don't, including many strong ones. The work still counts: your merged code, your reviews, and the maintainers who now know your name. Many people are selected on their second try, or join LFX, Outreachy or another program on a different calendar. See [if you're not selected](../if-not-selected/).
